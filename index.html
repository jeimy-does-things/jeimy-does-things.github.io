The ethos.

When I was writing, I wasn't quite sure of who I was. This is not to say "I was lost in this world". The situational array of ways that a person may go about their writing, that was where I didn't know: can I today be the one who talks about a crime from a city I no longer have the agency to presently describe?

To me, the solution, the thing that makes me a writer, is exactly that I am able to close that gap. Looking back to the showcased projects: I was successfully able to criticize my own cognition process and go through large revision and sentence weaving processes that led to a coherent end result. Where I compromised to sacrifice the stillness of research-level description (oftentime shallow against sole creativity) with personal experience and the humility of admitting the lack of information in some places. Instead of saying "hey, here is how the world looks like right now.", I managed to deconstruct down to "this is what the world was, and that which is invisible is the responsibility of us, the ones engaged in the reading and writing process."

The man behind the pen. A student, and human as well.

The pathos.

What makes language so attractive is that I could have very well be speaking exactly the same things I wrote about from under a well, or in a bank heist; where the action-place-person combo would have completely reinterpreted the meaning of the things said. What changes in the pathos for me is that the reader is the one who is now interpreting my words in their own voice. And I like to play with that; my writing isn't ethically perfect nor asking to take effective action of major problems, it's asking for the engagement of criticizing the placement of my own character, and everyone else's in the stories that I write. Such a character is also the one that the reader has built of the narrating voice, and as such, there is a silence call to explore whether the built-in voice in one's head has put together the right interpretation of the written word. That is, the world in my words is simple, but the entire emotional world and depth that I give to it has a basis on the subjectivity, the ways that essays will shock one when an unexpected turn gets braided.

Relatively simple things can create vast emotions and reactions. Such a principle applies to writing.

The logos.

> The opening of the portfolio showcased a self that is naive to writing, closed to thinking outside of the box with his words, and really shy about making things that weren't what weren't the consumption of ideas which already floated around his head.

> The continuing section of the portfolio is an opening, a minor scale revalation towards engagement with the community and people that saw me grow. It is much more interesting to write about abstract things when their application follows that the problem itself is a real problem.

> The middle of the portfolio showcases a little more creative flexibility, someone who is still attempting to land jabs at language but is excited to experiment with the things at stake on each project.

> The end for me, is writing humility. I wanted to show that I could write something that didn't have a massive, out of the world thesis. Instead, I wanted something that landed and had descriptions that were brutally accurate but imaginative and thoughtful; all of this without saying something that works as a moral patch to the world's problems. Which I believe I achieved.

Writing isn't rocket science, is it?

The SLOs

  • Implement and reflect upon composing processes that are individualized, flexible, and contextually responsive.

    In my mind, the reflection comes at the time of writing. And everytime that more articles are written, the more one starts to figure out the patterns of creation and composition in the outside when reading other texts.

  • Adapt composing practices to specific modalities, genres, social situations, and cultural contexts.

    One of my favorite projects was the one of writing reflections to braided essays that spoke about different communities, and different genres were showcased. I believe that it is important that one goes out in the field of writing with cultural awareness, but more importantly, with openness to go far beyond limits to understand the modes of living of people. All with the purpose of writing about them.

  • Demonstrate ethical practices in the collection and use of information.

    Doing MLA citations, and practices with research where we were able to compare the ethos, logos, and pathos were all helpful assignments that taught me a lot about information practices, and ethics. I struggled a lot with this initially, I am prone to hurrying and trying to select my information by reading the abstract and results, which is a catastrophic error when ethically sourcing data.

  • Suggest constructive global and local revisions to other writers.

    This is one of those things that were the most challenging to me. I happened to forget many times that we had discussions due, as they are not a normal mode of instruction in my math classes. Overall, giving out feedback to me is also hard because I get scared of being hurtful, or a bad sentence structure sounding like a harsh criticism instead of a passerby thought.

  • Utilize feedback to produce global and local revisions in their own writing.

    The professor's feedback was crucial for success in this course. Throughout the class, the writing tasks and their embedded sub tasks like making research and maps wouldn't have been nearly as cleanly made if I wouldn't have taken suggestions from the professor. As a matter of fact, at a certain point, I was sure I would have to generate new content everytime I wrote something, albeit having posted drafts in the assignments. Meaning that I was attempting to waste a lot of time recicling the same words in different ways.

  • Recognize and articulate how their values, goals, and/or lived experiences inform their choices as writers.

    I have lived a lot. Perhaps, a lot more than I should have lived at a short age. I like writing and revealing a lot of things in the subconcious that wouldn't have otherwise come out. In this SLO, I believe this is a strong indicator that the work I did was helpful deconstructing the individual thought, and using it as a strength when making decisions and self-criticism.

  • Use meaningful, multifaceted, ongoing reflection to identify how knowledge, skills, processes, and experiences can transfer across writing contexts.

    I believe that the most important example of this is my work regarding my culture, and my language. As a spanish speaking, it is hard to not think about certain words and sentence structures that are incompatible with the english language but completely valid to write about in spanish. My prior knowledge (or lack thereof in many cases) was the exact indicator of me having to work on something other than just engineering papers. To me, this task wasn't trivial, but I believe that it has been fruitful as I was very satisfied with my photo essay.

  • Identify and analyze how texts are influenced by their rhetorical situations, genres, and modalities.

    This is one SLO where I would also add analyzing the cultural relationships that the author of texts have. I found out through the course that the genres and rhetorical situations were all much more heavily influenced by their past experiences (which strictly correlate to their cultural experiences).

  • Analyze genres and their conventions within various rhetorical, cultural, and linguistic contexts.

    Which brings to this SLO. Which I would mix with the answer of the one above. In the case of genres, the problem of genre selection is easily given away by the unique experiences that people have lived.

  • Make purposeful and context-sensitive choices regarding language, structure, and design conventions.

    Throughout the course, I found myself battling with my necessity to use the dense language of math: "Therefore this implies that for which we X and thus we Y, insofar as case Z goes..." Having a class which challenged my writing conventions and the things that I have built overtime is a good refresh.

  • Support ideas through the use of examples, observations, experiences, and reasoning.

    Research was the most important example of this. Without research, almost anything to be said could have landed (and submitted as such). Doing research really permitted landing the right words with the right historical and social context, instead of parroting ideas without any arguments.

  • Read and analyze texts across multiple genres and rhetorical contexts.

    My favorite comparison was Boyer in comparison to Wideman. Where ones narrative had a much more quiet and firm pace, Boyer's essay was relentless and had a lot of shining arguments albeit of the grieving sentiment that the sentences had. There is a beauty in being able to tell the purposeful decisions in the style of writing from other people.

  • Develop and implement research strategies for focused inquiry.

    This was one of the SLOs where I struggled the most. I am good at research to the point of using it in science plenty of times. This doesn't mean that there was a set strategy, or that the questions asked were right 100% of the time. This was a major setback through all that required me to source information.

  • Evaluate sources for credibility, relevance, context, and accuracy.

    This one was an easy SLO due to linguistics. There is a hidden mechanism in analyzing the grammar of titles, sentences, and going through the chains of implications which lets one easily decipher whether information is written with a good foundation.

  • Synthesize varied research findings to develop and share ideas.

    During the time that I was doing a more science-focused research, this was an extremely hard task. There is a difference between validating and explaining statistical correlations and tests in comparison to making a deep analysis of which ideas and tools of a written argument are shared, and which ones disagree.

  • Identify and apply situationally appropriate practices for idea attribution.

    Doing citations was easy, and an effective way to attribute ideas to their corresponding authors. Although, with all due honesty, it is sometimes hard to identify when one is also reflecting someone's ideas through conversation: in which case citations go flying out of the window.
