Desertic writer's digest, untold stories from known places.

"The things you think you're useless I can't understand", the lyrics straight across from Steely Dan's string section, permeate the essence of my writing. I write with a passion for alienating from priorities. The stories I told all this semester are an exploration of the self and its context through the lens of trivial things: the witty and imaginative. Although the stylistic and content decisions have never been a struggle, there was a deeper culprit in the creative process: the capacity to let the human flourish in my writing.

I originally spoke Spanish, and I learnt to write and read in English from watching Pokemon game tutorials when I was 6 or 7 years old. Coming into this class, I was more sure I knew the names of fantastic videogame creatures than the one I would write on the title of each project. Follow my story around ENGL109H.

To accompany this portfolio, I decided to add the album that has carried me along all the assignments. Darondo's "Let my people go". The order of the works is chronological and meant to showcase the changes in the strengths and weaknesses of my writing throughout the semester, with captions explaining how they correlate with the general arc of learning and with each other.

The ethos.

When I was writing, I wasn't quite sure who I was. This is not to say "I was lost." The situational array of ways that a person may go about their writing was what I didn't know: can I today be the one who talks about a crime from a city I no longer have the agency to describe?

To me, the solution, the thing that makes me a writer, is that I am able to close that gap. Looking back at the showcased projects, I was able to criticize my own cognition process and go through large revision and sentence weaving processes that led to a coherent result. Where I compromised by sacrificing the stillness of research-level description (oftentimes shallow against sole creativity) for personal experience and the humility of admitting the lack of information in some places. Instead of saying "hey, here is how the world looks right now.", I managed to deconstruct to "this is what the world was, and that which is invisible is the responsibility of us, the ones engaged in the reading and writing process."

The man behind the pen. A student, and human as well.

The pathos.

What makes language so attractive is that I could very well have been speaking exactly the same things I wrote about from under a well, or in a bank heist, where the action-place-person combo would have completely reinterpreted the meaning of the things said. What changes in the pathos for me is that the reader is now interpreting my words in their own voice. And I like to play with that; my writing isn't ethically perfect nor does it ask to take effective action on major problems, it asks for the engagement of criticizing the placement of my own character, and everyone else's in the stories that I write. Such a character is also the one that the reader has built from the narrating voice, and as such, there is a silent call to explore whether the built-in voice in one's head has put together the right interpretation of the written word. That is, the world in my words is simple, but the entire emotional world and depth that I give to it has a basis in subjectivity, the ways that essays will shock one when an unexpected turn gets braided.

Relatively simple things can create vast emotions and reactions. Such a principle applies to writing.

The logos.

> The opening of the portfolio showcased a self that was naive to writing, closed to thinking outside the box with his words, and shy about making things that weren't the consumption of ideas which already floated around his head.

> The continuing section of the portfolio is an opening, a minor-scale revelation towards engagement with the community and people that saw me grow. It is much more interesting to write about abstract things when their application follows from the fact that the problem itself is a real problem.

> The middle of the portfolio showcases a little more creative flexibility, someone who is still attempting to land jabs at language but is excited to experiment with the things at stake on each project.

> The end for me is writing humility. I wanted to show that I could write something that didn't have a massive, out of the world thesis. Instead, I wanted something that landed and had descriptions that were brutally accurate but imaginative and thoughtful, all of this without saying something that works as a moral patch to the world's problems. Which I believe I achieved.

Writing isn't rocket science, is it?

The SLOs

  • Implement and reflect upon composing processes that are individualized, flexible, and contextually responsive.

    In my mind, the reflection comes at the time of writing. And every time more articles are written, the more one starts to figure out the patterns of creation and composition in the outside when reading other texts.

  • Adapt composing practices to specific modalities, genres, social situations, and cultural contexts.

    One of my favorite projects was writing reflections on braided essays that spoke about different communities, and different genres were showcased. I believe that it is important that one goes out into the field of writing with cultural awareness, but more importantly, with openness to go far beyond limits to understand the modes of living of people. All with the purpose of writing about them.

  • Demonstrate ethical practices in the collection and use of information.

    Doing MLA citations and practicing research where we were able to compare the ethos, logos, and pathos were all helpful assignments that taught me a lot about information practices and ethics. I struggled a lot with this initially. I am prone to hurrying and trying to select my information by reading the abstract and results, which is a catastrophic error when ethically sourcing data.

  • Suggest constructive global and local revisions to other writers.

    This is one of those things that was the most challenging to me. I happened to forget many times that we had discussions due, as they are not a normal mode of instruction in my math classes. Overall, giving feedback to me is also hard because I get scared of being hurtful, or a bad sentence structure sounding like a harsh criticism instead of a passerby thought.

  • Utilize feedback to produce global and local revisions in their own writing.

    The professor's feedback was crucial for success in this course. Throughout the class, the writing tasks and their embedded subtasks like making research and maps wouldn't have been nearly as cleanly made if I wouldn't have taken suggestions from the professor. As a matter of fact, at a certain point, I was sure I would have to generate new content every time I wrote something, despite having posted drafts in the assignments. Meaning that I was attempting to waste a lot of time recycling the same words in different ways.

  • Recognize and articulate how their values, goals, and/or lived experiences inform their choices as writers.

    I have lived a lot. Perhaps, a lot more than I should have lived at a short age. I like writing and revealing a lot of things in the subconscious that wouldn't have otherwise come out. In this SLO, I believe this is a strong indicator that the work I did was helpful in deconstructing the individual thought, and using it as a strength when making decisions and self-criticism.

  • Use meaningful, multifaceted, ongoing reflection to identify how knowledge, skills, processes, and experiences can transfer across writing contexts.

    I believe that the most important example of this is my work regarding my culture and my language. As a Spanish speaker, it is hard to not think about certain words and sentence structures that are incompatible with the English language but completely valid to write about in Spanish. My prior knowledge (or lack thereof in many cases) was the exact indicator of me having to work on something other than just engineering papers. To me, this task wasn't trivial, but I believe that it has been fruitful as I was very satisfied with my photo essay.

  • Identify and analyze how texts are influenced by their rhetorical situations, genres, and modalities.

    This is one SLO where I would also add analyzing the cultural relationships that the authors of texts have. I found out through the course that the genres and rhetorical situations were all much more heavily influenced by their past experiences (which directly correlate with their cultural experiences).

  • Analyze genres and their conventions within various rhetorical, cultural, and linguistic contexts.

    Which brings me to this SLO. Which I would mix with the answer to the one above. In the case of genres, the problem of genre selection is easily given away by the unique experiences that people have lived.

  • Make purposeful and context-sensitive choices regarding language, structure, and design conventions.

    Throughout the course, I found myself battling with my necessity to use the dense language of math: "Therefore this implies that for which we X and thus we Y, insofar as case Z goes..." Having a class which challenged my writing conventions and the things that I have built over time is a good refresh.

  • Support ideas through the use of examples, observations, experiences, and reasoning.

    Research was the most important example of this. Without research, almost anything to be said could have landed and been submitted as such. Doing research permitted landing the right words with the right historical and social context, instead of parroting ideas without any arguments.

  • Read and analyze texts across multiple genres and rhetorical contexts.

    My favorite comparison was Boyer in comparison to Wideman. Where one's narrative had a quieter and firmer pace, Boyer's essay was relentless and had a lot of shining arguments, albeit with the grieving sentiment that the sentences had. There is a beauty in being able to tell the purposeful decisions in the style of writing from other people.

  • Develop and implement research strategies for focused inquiry.

    This was one of the SLOs where I struggled the most. I am good at research to the point of using it in science plenty of times. This doesn't mean that there was a set strategy, or that the questions asked were right 100% of the time. This was a major setback through all that required me to source information.

  • Evaluate sources for credibility, relevance, context, and accuracy.

    This one was an easy SLO due to linguistics. There is a hidden mechanism in analyzing the grammar of titles and sentences, and going through the chains of implications lets one more easily decipher whether information is written with a good foundation.

  • Synthesize varied research findings to develop and share ideas.

    During the time that I was doing more science-focused research, this was an extremely hard task. There is a difference between validating and explaining statistical correlations and tests in comparison to making a deep analysis of which ideas and tools of a written argument are shared, and which ones disagree.

  • Identify and apply situationally appropriate practices for idea attribution.

    Doing citations was easy, and an effective way to attribute ideas to their corresponding authors. Although, with all due honesty, it is sometimes hard to identify when one is also reflecting someone's ideas through conversation: in which case citations go flying out of the window.

Conclusion

All good stories have to come to an end. It was an honor being part of the class, as much as it was an honor being exposed to the beautiful works of my peers. I hope that the general character arc for myself in writing ends up being the one of a man who loves to write, who had given up creative writing for formalism, and ended up reconnecting with it in the form of complex essay structures and talking about the places that matter the most to him. Special thank you to the professor for such a great course.
